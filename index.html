The "Killer Question" toolkit (reuse in any session)
- The numbers question: "What is the cost per kWp / per m³ of biogas / per ton of waste, and what is the payback period?"
- The failure question: "What did not work, and what did you change?" (Failures teach more than success stories.)
- The "who pays" question: "Who finances it, who operates it, and who pays when the subsidy ends?"
- The scaling question: "What works in a pilot but breaks at 100× scale?"
- The trade-off question: "What did you give up to achieve this? (cost, land, jobs, security of supply)"
- The transfer question: "Which parts of this model would not work in a tropical archipelago like Indonesia, and why?"
- The framing opener: "In Indonesia we face X. How did China deal with X, and what would you do differently today?"

Discussion etiquette
- Be curious and respectful: ask how and why, not "why did you fail". Frame challenging questions as comparisons ("In Indonesia we struggle with... how does it work here?").
- Never criticize politics. Use the policy-neutral form: "What trade-offs did this policy involve?"
- Ask one question at a time, then listen. Follow up on the answer.

D. 💬 Challenging discussion questions: Lecture 1 (China's national conditions)

Q1. Coal versus climate commitments
Context: China remains the world's largest coal consumer and keeps approving coal capacity for supply security, while adding hundreds of gigawatts of wind and solar every year. Official language describes this as "building the new before breaking the old" (先立后破). Indonesia faces the same tension between a coal-heavy system (including growing captive plants) and its Net Zero 2060 goal.
Ask: How do Chinese planners reconcile energy security with the 2030/2060 commitments, and what concrete milestones exist for coal use and coal capacity?

Q2. From national targets to local action
Context: Five-Year Plans set national goals that provinces and counties convert into local targets, project approvals and budgets. Indonesia has a similar chain (national energy policy and RUEN down to regional RUED), but implementation gaps are common, for example the renewable share target that was not met.
Ask: What happens when a province misses its energy or emissions target, and which incentives or penalties actually change local behavior?

Q3. The price of speed
Context: Large Chinese infrastructure projects often go from decision to construction very quickly, which helped renewables scale at record pace. In Indonesia, land acquisition, forest-area permits and PPA negotiations frequently add years to the same type of project.
Ask: What are the trade-offs of this speed in terms of land rights, community consultation and environmental review, and which procedural elements could be adopted without losing safeguards?

Q4. Rural energy as development policy
Context: Rural revitalization (乡村振兴) is a national agenda, and village-level energy such as biogas and solar is part of it. Indonesia has a comparable aim through programs such as Desa Mandiri Energi and electrification of frontier, outermost and underdeveloped (3T) areas.
Ask: Is cheap clean energy at the village level treated mainly as an economic-development tool or as an environmental one, and how does that affect budgets and success measures?

Q5. State-owned versus private roles
Context: In China, large state-owned groups dominate grids and large generation projects, while private firms lead manufacturing of solar panels, batteries and wind equipment. In Indonesia, PLN dominates the grid, independent power producers build plants under PLN contracts, and the sovereign investment body Danantara is becoming a new player.
Ask: How are risks and returns shared between state enterprises and private firms in China's energy transition, and what would be a workable division of roles in Indonesia?

E. 🇮🇩 Indonesia/MoEMR link

Tomorrow: Field Visit 1: Dujiangyan, Water Energy Efficient Utilization & Carbon Neutralization · Cultural Experience 1: Chengdu Giant Panda Garden (in Dujiangyan).

A. Key points: Dujiangyan (≈45 min)

  • History: built around 256 BC under Li Bing (李冰), Qin dynasty. No dam: it works with the river, not against it, and has operated for more than 2,200 years. UNESCO World Heritage (2000), together with Mount Qingcheng.
  • Three core components:
    1. Yuzui (鱼嘴, "fish mouth"): a levee splitting the Min River into an inner channel (irrigation) and an outer channel (flood).
    2. Feishayan (飞沙堰, "flying sand weir"): spills floodwater and sediment.
    3. Baopingkou (宝瓶口, "bottle-neck channel"): regulates flow to the Chengdu Plain.
  • Principles: work with nature, sediment management, simultaneous flood control, irrigation and transport. It turned the Chengdu Plain into the "land of abundance" (天府之国).
  • Energy link: Sichuan is China's largest hydropower province (>100 GW, including Baihetan ≈16 GW and Xiluodu ≈13.9 GW on the Jinsha River). It is a base for "West-to-East power transmission" (西电东送).
  • Water–energy–food nexus: water, energy and food are interdependent. Use this as the lens for the visit.

B. Key points: water efficiency & carbon neutrality (≈30 min)

  • Hydropower types: run-of-river vs reservoir; mini/micro-hydro (relevant to Indonesian villages: PLTMH); hydropower capacity factor ≈40–50%.
  • Carbon neutrality: emission sources, sinks (forest, soil, ocean), offsets, carbon markets. China launched its national ETS in 2021 for the power sector (later extended to steel, cement, aluminium). Indonesia has IDXCarbon (carbon exchange, 2023).

C. Panda Garden (≈15 min)

  • Giant panda: IUCN Vulnerable (upgraded from Endangered in 2016); wild population ≈1,800+; habitat in Sichuan, Shaanxi, Gansu. A flagship of ecological civilization, which is the theme of Monday.
  • Indonesia connection: two pandas (Cai Tao and Hu Chun) have lived at Taman Safari Indonesia, Cisarua, since 2017.

D. 💬 Challenging discussion questions: Field Visit 1 (Dujiangyan)

Q1. The institutional secret
Context: Dujiangyan has worked for roughly 2,200 years, and tradition attributes its upkeep to rules of annual maintenance such as deep dredging and low weirs (深淘滩，低作堰). Engineering alone does not explain such longevity; governance and maintenance culture matter as much.
Ask: What is the institutional secret behind the system's survival: engineering, governance, or maintenance culture, and how is upkeep financed today?

Q2. Allocation under drought
Context: In summer 2022, record heat and drought in Sichuan cut hydropower output sharply and forced power rationing for industry. Sichuan depends on hydropower for the large majority of its electricity.
Ask: When water is scarce, who decides how it is shared among irrigation, cities, industry and hydropower, and on what basis?

Q3. Resilience of a hydro-dominated grid
Context: Droughts and heat waves show the risk of relying on a single weather-dependent source, even a renewable one. Indonesia's hydropower is similarly exposed to El Niño dry years.
Ask: What is the backup plan for a hydro-dominated system (solar, storage, interconnection, coal), and how is the cost of that backup shared?

Q4. Dam-free, nature-based design
Context: Dujiangyan diverts water without a large dam, so it does not trap sediment or block fish migration the way modern reservoirs do. Modern hydropower, including large Chinese and Indonesian dams, faces criticism on sediment, ecosystems and resettlement.
Ask: Should nature-based design inform new hydropower today, or does it not scale to modern power demand?

Q5. Claims of carbon neutrality at a site
Context: Tourist sites and local governments increasingly announce "carbon neutral" claims, but accounting boundaries differ widely. Indonesia is building its own rules under the carbon economic value regulation (Perpres 98/2021) and the IDXCarbon exchange.
Ask: How is carbon neutrality measured and claimed at a site like this, and what counts as a real reduction versus double counting?

E. 🇮🇩 Indonesia/MoEMR link

  • Hydropower in Indonesia: Cirata, Saguling, Jatiluhur, Kayan (planned, North Kalimantan), Batang Toru (controversial), plus PLTMH programs in remote villages. Indonesian hydropower output is exposed to El Niño drought. Ask how Sichuan's 2022 experience should shape RUPTL planning.
  • Traditional water governance: Subak in Bali (UNESCO 2012) is the closest parallel: a community institution managing irrigation sustainably. Ask: "How can community water institutions co-manage micro-hydro?"
  • Multi-purpose reservoirs: the Citarum cascade serves power, water supply and irrigation; compare allocation rules with Dujiangyan's.
  • Panda/eco-tourism analogy: conservation funded by tourism is also Indonesia's challenge (Komodo, orangutan, Taman Safari). Ask: "How is eco-tourism revenue linked to protecting the resource?"
  • Carbon market: compare to Indonesia's Perpres 98/2021 on carbon economic value (NEK) and IDXCarbon.

A. Ecological Civilization / 生态文明 (≈40 min)

  • Written into the constitution (2018) and national strategy. Goal: "Beautiful China" (美丽中国).
  • Key slogan: "Lucid waters and lush mountains are invaluable assets" (绿水青山就是金山银山): protecting the environment is protecting economic assets. Originated around 2005 in Anji, Zhejiang.
  • Six principles (from the 2018 national environmental conference):
    1. Harmony between humanity and nature
    2. Lucid waters and lush mountains are invaluable assets
    3. A good ecological environment is the most inclusive public welfare
    4. Mountains, rivers, forests, farmland, lakes, grassland and sand as a community of life (山水林田湖草沙)
    5. Strictest institutions and rule of law
    6. A global community with a shared future
  • Instruments: ecological red lines, ecological compensation, carbon market, central environmental inspections, air and water quality targets.
  • How to approach it: this is a policy/ideology lecture. Capture the framework and the concrete instruments; then compare them with Indonesia: Law 32/2009 (environmental protection), RPJMN, NDC, Net Zero Emission 2060.

B. China's green energy strategy & policy (≈45 min)

  • Structure: Five-Year Plan → sectoral renewable plans → regulations → markets.
  • Milestones:
  • Renewable Energy Law (2006): grid purchase obligation, feed-in tariffs (FiT).
  • Large subsidies 2009–2020 → PV/wind boom; then transition to subsidy-free (grid parity) and market-based pricing (2025 pricing reform, "Document 136").
  • Dual Carbon (2030/2060). 2030 targets include ≈25% non-fossil share and ≥1,200 GW wind + solar (reached early, in 2024).
  • Energy Law (in force 2025) codifies energy priorities.
  • 2035 commitments (new NDC announced in 2025): net emissions cut ≈7–10% from peak, non-fossil share >30%, wind + solar ≈3,600 GW (verify).
  • "Build the new before breaking the old" (先立后破): add clean energy while keeping supply secure; no sudden coal closures.
  • Rural: distributed rooftop PV, biogas, clean heating, "rural energy revolution".

C. 💬 Challenging discussion questions: Lecture 2 (Ecological Civilization)

Q1. When targets collide
Context: Ecological civilization is written into China's constitution and national strategy, and environmental indicators are part of how local officials are evaluated. In practice, environmental goals sometimes collide with local growth and employment targets. Indonesia sees similar conflicts between mining or plantation interests and environmental protection.
Ask: When environmental and growth targets conflict locally, which one wins in practice, and which mechanisms (cadre evaluation, central inspections, red lines) enforce the outcome?

Q2. Ecological red lines
Context: China has designated "ecological red lines", protected zones that cover a large share of national territory (reported at around a quarter, verify). Restricting land use creates costs for farmers and local governments. Indonesia has comparable tools such as forest-area status, moratoria and spatial plans.
Ask: How are ecological red lines defined, enforced and compensated, and who bears the cost of land that cannot be developed?

Q3. Nature as an asset
Context: The slogan "lucid waters and lush mountains are invaluable assets" treats ecosystems as economic assets, supported by ecological compensation schemes, for example between upstream and downstream provinces along a river. Indonesia is exploring payments for ecosystem services and carbon markets.
Ask: Does putting a price on ecosystems risk commodifying nature, and how does China manage that risk?

Q4. What can and cannot be transferred
Context: Some features of the Chinese approach rely on centralized authority and a particular political system, while others (indicator frameworks, compensation schemes, inspection teams) are institutional tools. Indonesia is a decentralized democracy where regional governments hold many permitting powers.
Ask: Which parts of the ecological civilization approach are cultural or political and cannot be transferred, and which could work in a decentralized country like Indonesia?

Q5. Measuring results
Context: A policy is only as credible as its indicators. Governments that publish consistent, verifiable data (air quality, forest cover, emissions) tend to sustain public trust.
Ask: Is there a published, independently verifiable indicator set for ecological civilization, and how often are targets revised?

D. 💬 Challenging discussion questions: Lecture 3 (Green Energy Strategy & Policy)

Q1. Exiting subsidies
Context: China moved from generous feed-in tariffs to subsidy-free projects and, in 2025, to market-based pricing for new renewables (often called Document 136). Investors had to adjust to revenue that depends on market prices and contract mechanisms. Indonesia's renewable ceiling prices under Perpres 112/2022 are a different model with different risks.
Ask: What happened to investor confidence and project returns during this transition, and what safeguards were built in?

Q2. Curtailment and the real bottleneck
Context: Despite record installations, some wind and solar output is curtailed, with national utilization rates in the mid-to-high 90s percent but lower in certain northwestern provinces (verify). Indonesia's Java–Bali grid and outer-island systems face their own integration limits.
Ask: Is the main bottleneck now transmission, storage, market design or dispatch rules, and which fix has delivered the most so far?

Q3. The carbon price signal
Context: China's national emissions trading system launched in 2021 for the power sector and has since expanded to more industries, but prices have been far lower than in Europe (verify current level). Indonesia is in the early stages of its own carbon trading and carbon tax design.
Ask: Is the ETS meant to drive investment or mainly to build market infrastructure, and how will the cap be tightened over time?

Q4. A just transition for coal regions
Context: Provinces such as Shanxi and Inner Mongolia depend heavily on coal jobs and revenues. Indonesia has the same issue in East Kalimantan and South Sumatra, and the JETP and early-retirement plans raise the question of who pays.
Ask: What is the exit plan for coal workers and coal-dependent provinces, and what funding supports it?

Q5. Industrial policy and trade
Context: China dominates the supply chains for solar panels, batteries and wind turbines, which lowers global costs but concentrates risk. Indonesia wants local manufacturing and applies local-content rules (TKDN), which can raise project costs.
Ask: Was this dominance an intended strategy, and what does it imply for countries such as Indonesia that want local value added without slowing deployment?

Q6. Learning from failure
Context: Every long-running policy program has surprises, for example boom-and-bust cycles in solar subsidies or unused rural digesters. Policy-makers learn the most from those cases.
Ask: Which policy has been the biggest failure or surprise in the last 15 years, and what was changed as a result?

A. Biogas technology basics (≈60 min)

  • Anaerobic digestion (AD), 4 stages: hydrolysis → acidogenesis → acetogenesis → methanogenesis.
  • Biogas composition: CH₄ 50–70%, CO₂ 30–45%, traces of H₂S, NH₃, water vapor. Heating value ≈20–25 MJ/m³. Rule of thumb: 1 m³ biogas ≈ 0.6–0.7 m³ natural gas ≈ 1.5–2 kWh electricity (via gas engine).
  • Operating parameters: temperature (mesophilic ≈35 °C, thermophilic ≈55 °C), pH 6.8–7.5, C/N ratio ≈20–30:1, HRT (hydraulic retention time), OLR (organic loading rate), TS/VS, inhibitors (ammonia, H₂S, volatile fatty acids).
  • Digester types: fixed-dome (the Chinese hydraulic model, 水压式沼气池), floating drum (India), tubular/plastic bag (Latin America), CSTR, UASB, plug flow, covered lagoon (POME).
  • Products: biogas (cooking, power, CHP), biomethane (>95% CH₄ via water scrubbing, PSA, membranes, amine), digestate (solid/liquid organic fertilizer).

B. History & Chinese biogas models (≈30 min)

  • Mass rural biogas began in the 1970s, with Sichuan a pioneer. At its peak, China had tens of millions of household digesters (≈40 million+): the world's largest rural biogas program.
  • Policy phases: campaign era (1970s) → standards & subsidies (2000s) → large/medium-scale and communal plants, then biomethane/bio-CNG (2010s onward).
  • Integrated models: "three-in-one" pig–biogas–orchard (south) and "four-in-one" greenhouse–pigsty–biogas–toilet (north). Biogas is the hub of the farm cycle.
  • Challenges: operation & maintenance, stable feedstock, unused gas, business viability, digestate management.
  • Indonesia link: the BIRU (Biogas Rumah) program (Hivos/SNV), since 2009, built tens of thousands of digesters; POME biogas at palm oil mills; large potential from cattle, pigs and poultry.

C. Biomass (≈30 min)

  • Sources: agricultural residues (straw, husk, bagasse), forest residues, livestock manure, organic waste, energy crops, used cooking oil.
  • Conversion routes:
  • Thermochemical: direct combustion, gasification, pyrolysis/biochar, torrefaction.
  • Biochemical: AD, fermentation (bioethanol).
  • Physical/chemical: pellets/briquettes, biodiesel (transesterification), HVO/SAF.
  • China: straw ≈700–800 million tons/year with comprehensive utilization >85%; biomass power capacity in the tens of GW; open-burning bans.
  • Indonesia: biodiesel B35 → B40 (2025) → planned B50, biomass co-firing at PLN coal plants, potential from palm, sugarcane, rice husk and energy forestry.

D. 💬 Challenging discussion questions: Lecture 4 (Rural biogas)

Q1. Why digesters were abandoned
Context: China built tens of millions of household digesters, but studies in several countries and periods found a significant share idle because of poor construction, lack of feedstock or lack of maintenance. Indonesia's BIRU household program has faced similar sustainability questions.
Ask: What were the top three causes of abandonment in China, and which policy changes reversed the trend?

Q2. From household to communal and large plants
Context: Over the last 15 years, support shifted toward medium and large biogas plants at livestock farms and centralized systems. Household digesters declined as rural people migrated to cities and switched to LPG or electricity.
Ask: What was gained (efficiency, professional operation) and lost (ownership, simplicity) in this shift?

Q3. Choosing the best pathway
Context: Biogas can be burned directly, converted to electricity, or upgraded to biomethane for gas grids or vehicles. The best option depends on gas prices, grid access and incentives. Indonesia's city gas network and PLN tariffs for small renewables shape the same decision.
Ask: Which pathway gives the best value per ton of manure today, and what assumptions drive that answer?

Q4. The digestate problem
Context: A large pig farm produces large volumes of liquid digestate that need land for application. Without enough land, digestate becomes a pollution problem rather than a fertilizer.
Ask: How is digestate processed, sold or applied, and who is responsible for heavy metals, antibiotics and pathogen risks?

Q5. Methane leakage
Context: Methane has a global warming potential of roughly 27–30 times that of CO₂ over 100 years, so even small leaks can offset climate benefits. Reliable monitoring is rare in small and rural plants. Indonesia faces the same issue with methane from POME ponds and livestock.
Ask: How are leaks measured and regulated, and are digesters counted in carbon accounting or offsets?

Q6. Standards and training
Context: BIOMA works as a research institute, standards body and training provider for biogas. Quality across thousands of builders and operators depends on this system.
Ask: How does the standards and training system keep quality high at scale, and could it serve as a regional hub for ASEAN countries including Indonesia?

E. 💬 Challenging discussion questions: Lecture 5 (Biomass)

Q1. Food, feed or fuel
Context: Biomass energy can compete with food production and land for other uses. Indonesia has faced this debate over palm oil for biodiesel and cassava or sugarcane for ethanol.
Ask: How does China prevent biomass energy from competing with food security, and what rules protect land use?

Q2. Straw logistics
Context: Straw has low density and is available seasonally, so collection, storage and transport can cost more than the energy value. Many biomass plants struggle with fuel supply costs.
Ask: What business model (collection stations, long-term contracts, pelletizing) actually works, and what is the delivered fuel cost per ton?

Q3. Life after subsidies
Context: Central subsidies for new biomass power were largely wound down after 2020 (verify), and plants had to rely on local support or other revenue. Indonesia's biomass tariffs are relatively low compared with fuel costs.
Ask: Which biomass plants survived without subsidies and which closed, and why?

Q4. Open-burning bans
Context: China restricts burning crop straw in fields to reduce air pollution, which creates a need for alternative uses. Indonesia has similar issues with burning of rice straw and agricultural residues.
Ask: How are bans enforced, and what income do farmers receive for straw instead of burning it?

Q5. Co-firing: transition tool or coal life-extension
Context: Co-firing biomass in coal plants cuts emissions at low capital cost, but it can also prolong the life of coal assets. PLN has co-fired biomass pellets at dozens of coal plants, and sustainability verification of the fuel is a concern.
Ask: Is co-firing a genuine transition tool, and what sustainability certification applies?

Q6. Biofuel mandates and land use
Context: Mandating blended biofuels creates stable demand but raises questions of land use and food prices. Indonesia's biodiesel blend has risen from B35 to B40 with a plan toward B50 and is financed by a levy on palm oil exports (BPDP).
